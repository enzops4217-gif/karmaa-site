Mouse polling rate: 1000 vs 4000 vs 8000 Hz

Free guideUpdated November 22, 20265 min read

Gaming mice now go up to 8000 Hz. Higher sounds better, but the gain is measured in fractions of a millisecond and it has a cost on your processor. Here is what each value really changes, and how to choose for your PC and your monitor.

What polling rate means

The polling rate is how often the mouse reports its position to the PC, in reports per second (Hz). The time between two reports is simply 1 divided by the rate:

Polling rateInterval between reportsAverage extra delay
125 Hz8 msabout 4 ms
500 Hz2 msabout 1 ms
1000 Hz1 msabout 0.5 ms
2000 Hz0.5 msabout 0.25 ms
4000 Hz0.25 msabout 0.125 ms
8000 Hz0.125 msabout 0.06 ms

The “average extra delay” is half the interval: a movement happens at a random moment and waits on average half a cycle before being sent. Going from 1000 to 8000 Hz therefore saves under half a millisecond on average. That is real but small compared to total system latency, which is usually 15 to 40 ms (see our input lag guide).

The real benefit: smoother motion on fast monitors

The bigger gain is not raw latency but consistency. On a 240, 360 or 500 Hz monitor, each frame lasts 2–4 ms. At 1000 Hz, a frame receives a variable number of mouse reports (for example 2 or 3 at 360 Hz), which can cause slight micro-stutter when you track a target. At 4000 or 8000 Hz, every frame gets many reports, so cursor and camera motion look smoother. On a 60 or 144 Hz monitor, you will hardly see a difference above 1000 Hz.

The cost: CPU load and compatibility

Each report is a USB interrupt that Windows and the game must process. At 8000 Hz, that is eight times more work than at 1000 Hz.

  • CPU usage: on a recent CPU it is a few percent, but on older or busy CPUs it can lower FPS or cause stutter, especially in CPU-bound games.
  • Game support: most modern games using raw input handle 8000 Hz well. Some older games or engines stutter or lose FPS when the mouse moves fast. If a game behaves badly, lower the rate for that game.
  • USB setup: high rates are sensitive to hubs, front panel ports and shared controllers. Use a rear motherboard port (see our USB port guide).
  • Wireless battery: at 4000 or 8000 Hz, battery life can drop a lot. Check your mouse maker’s figures.
  • Sensor movement: to send 8000 different positions per second, the mouse must move fast enough and at a high enough DPI. At low DPI and slow movement, many reports are identical. Some makers recommend 1600 DPI or more at 8000 Hz.

Which one to choose

Your setupGood choice
60–144 Hz monitor, any CPU1000 Hz
240 Hz monitor, mid-range CPU1000 or 2000 Hz
240–360 Hz monitor, recent CPU2000 or 4000 Hz
360–500 Hz monitor, high-end CPU, competitive shooters4000 or 8000 Hz, after testing
Older CPU, CPU-bound games, or stutter at high rates1000 Hz
Wireless and battery life matters1000 or 2000 Hz

For many players, 2000 to 4000 Hz is the sweet spot: most of the smoothness gain without the full CPU cost of 8000 Hz.

How to change it

  1. Open your mouse software (Logitech G HUB, Razer Synapse, Pulsar, Lamzu, Zowie, etc.) or the maker’s web driver.
  2. Find the Polling rate or Report rate setting.
  3. For wireless mice, 4000 and 8000 Hz often need a specific dongle (sometimes sold separately). Plug it in a rear USB port.
  4. Some mice save the rate in their memory; others need the software to stay installed. Check after a restart.

How to test your polling rate

Do not trust the box: check what your PC actually receives.

  • Use a polling rate tester (online or in an app) and move the mouse quickly in circles. The reading should stay close to the chosen value. Large drops point at a USB, cable, dongle or CPU issue.
  • Note that browsers can cap or smooth the readings, especially above 1000 Hz. A native Windows tester is more reliable at 4000–8000 Hz.
  • Then test in-game: same map, same scenario, at 1000 Hz and at your higher rate. Watch the FPS, 1% lows and how tracking feels.

Related Windows settings

  • Enhance pointer precision: turn it off in Control Panel → Mouse → Pointer Options. It is acceleration, not precision.
  • Windows pointer speed: keep the slider on the 6th notch (the default, 1:1). Change sensitivity in your mouse software (DPI) or in the game instead.
  • Raw input: turn it on in games that offer it, so the game reads the mouse directly.
  • USB selective suspend: on some PCs, setting it to Disabled in the power plan’s advanced settings prevents a sleeping dongle or mouse from waking up late. Its effect is usually small on desktops.

Common myths

  • “Higher is always better.” Not if your CPU, game or USB port cannot keep up. A stable 2000 Hz beats an 8000 Hz that drops to 3000 Hz every time you flick.
  • “8000 Hz makes you aim better.” It makes motion smoother and slightly more responsive. Your aim still depends on sensitivity, practice and a stable frame rate.
  • “Polling rate and DPI are the same thing.” DPI is how far the cursor moves per inch of mouse movement. Polling rate is how often the mouse reports that movement. Changing one does not change the other.
  • “Windows controls it.” Windows has no polling rate setting for modern gaming mice: it lives in the mouse firmware, set by the maker’s software.

In short

  • 1000 → 8000 Hz saves under 0.5 ms on average: real, but small.
  • The visible benefit is smoother tracking on 240 Hz+ monitors.
  • The cost is CPU load, game compatibility and battery life.
  • 2000–4000 Hz is a good compromise for most; 8000 Hz only if your CPU and games handle it.
  • Always test the real rate and your FPS before keeping a setting.
